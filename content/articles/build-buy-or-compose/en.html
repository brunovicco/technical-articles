Itaú's IARA case helps frame a choice that comes before the framework: which capabilities should stay under the organization's control, and which can be contracted or combined without compromising operations?

In July 2026, in an interview with EXAME, Itaú's then-CTO Ricardo Guerra described the internal platform called IARA, which acts as a gateway to several language models, including market options and proprietary engines developed with NeoSpace. The choice of engine depends on the interaction; for certain operations, the bank also uses internal processing without calling an external LLM.

In October, a case study published by OpenAI described IARA as a governance and orchestration layer that makes models available to Itaú's teams, with cost controls, observability and responsible AI policies. It cited a legal solution backed by more than 90 million documents, running more than 2 million tasks per month, and efficiency gains of 20% to 80% in steps of the area's processes. These figures are results reported in the study, not an independent measure of the platform's financial return.

The experience is interesting because it puts the build, buy or compose discussion under real conditions: many users, different types of task, sensitive data and a need for governance. From public information, one can observe a combination of internal components and third-party services. It is not possible to reconstruct the full internal design, calculate IARA's TCO, or claim this would be the best choice for another organization.

Still, the case helps formulate the decision a company must make before contracting its next agent platform.

The unit of decision is the capability, not the whole platform

A company plans to create agents for customer service, operations and document analysis. It can buy a ready-made product for one of the processes and build the others. It can also build a shared integration and authorization layer while contracting the runtime and inference.

The problem appears when the evaluation is run as a single choice: use the vendor's platform or do everything in-house. That framing mixes responsibilities with very different life cycles and risks.

An enterprise AI capability usually brings together model access, orchestration, context retrieval, tools, identity, authorization, evaluation, observability, interface and operations. A vendor contract can cover several of these parts, but it hardly eliminates the decisions that depend on the business domain.

The TOGAF Standard, 10th Edition, is a useful reference for organizing this work. Its enterprise architecture approach favors relating business capabilities, systems, information and technology before defining an implementation path. This does not mean applying the whole method to every agent: it means making the chosen architecture answer a known need, rather than merely the availability of a service.

Buy: when proximity to the process outweighs the lower freedom

Buying is usually attractive when a product already solves much of the expected flow. The initial benefit tends to appear in deployment, infrastructure administration and the set of available integrations.

A customer-service agent inside a widely used CRM, for example, can inherit objects, processes and channels that a separate implementation would need to rebuild. If the rules are predictable and the need for differentiation is small, that reuse can justify a commercial solution even with a higher variable cost.

The trade-off appears when the process stops fitting the product's extension points. The company then chooses between adapting the operation, creating a parallel layer or accepting a limitation. Dependencies can also spread through tool representations, conversation history, the permission model and event contracts.

There is also commercial risk: different billing metrics make a superficial comparison misleading. One vendor charges per user; another, per execution; another, per token or action. What looks cheap per call can cost more per request actually resolved. Discounts, minimum commitments, limits and data export must be part of the negotiation.

Buy is a strong candidate when the process is not very differentiating, the product meets the mandatory requirements and the company accepts the resulting dependency. This evaluation must include the ability to exit, not only the ease of entry.

Build: the control you gain and the operation you take on

Build also needs to be delimited. Building an application on models consumed through an API is different from operating an inference cluster with proprietary or open models. Likewise, implementing a workflow in LangGraph does not oblige the team to build the whole hosting, identity and observability service.

In-house construction is justified when the business logic truly differentiates the solution, when execution requirements are not met by available platforms, or when the domain demands explicit control over steps and external effects.

It brings an ongoing commitment: maintaining adapters, evolving interfaces, managing versions, security, testing, availability and incident handling. On a platform used by several areas, the team also takes on support and the evolution of shared contracts.

If the company decides to host its own models, it takes on compute capacity, GPU utilization, scaling, weight and pipeline updates, serving, quality and infrastructure operations. Fixed costs can be amortized by volume, but that gain depends on sufficient demand and effective use of the capacity.

The economic risk of build appears when the investment is justified by the expected number of applications while actual utilization stays low. Maintaining an internal platform can end up consuming more effort than the features it was meant to accelerate.

Compose: separating responsibility from the way of execution

The advance of agent services has made the composed alternative more practical. AWS documents Amazon Bedrock AgentCore as a modular set with runtime, gateway, memory, identity and other capabilities that can be used with different frameworks and models. Microsoft Foundry Agent Service distinguishes configuration-defined agents from hosted agents whose code is still developed by the customer. Google Cloud describes the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform as a set of tools to build, run, govern and evaluate agents. And Databricks Agent Bricks brings together runtime, durable execution and tracing integrated with the data environment.

These products do not offer exactly the same thing, nor do they share the same economic model. They show, however, that a company can choose where to write its code and where to delegate infrastructure, without necessarily contracting an indivisible package.

In a composed design, I would keep application contracts for tool access, authorization, structured inputs and outputs, and evidence. The runtime could be managed; inference, contracted; business logic, kept in the application's domain. That would allow certain components to be replaced without handing authority over the process to the vendor.

But compose has its own cost. Execution crosses more boundaries of trust, observability and failure. If two systems hold different versions of the same state, recovery may repeat work. If the access policy is enforced in only one gateway, a second route may produce a bypass. If telemetry does not preserve business identifiers, investigating an incident requires rebuilding the history by hand.

There is also lock-in in a modular architecture. Open protocols can reduce communication coupling, but they do not guarantee compatibility of state, policy or behavior across runtimes. Portability must be tested against a real ability to substitute.

What the public cases allow us to conclude

At Itaú, the EXAME interview describes its own logic for choosing between models, using commercial models, proprietary engines and internal processing for certain transactions. The study published by OpenAI adds the dimension of governance, quality, cost and observability in IARA. This supports reading it as a combined approach, but it does not reveal which components were bought, what they cost or which alternatives were discarded.

A second example helps show the diversity of combinations. In GenAds, Mercado Libre worked with Mutt Data and used AWS services, including Bedrock, S3 and DynamoDB, in a flow that generates advertising assets. AWS reports that the initiative produced more than 90 thousand creatives and reports differences in impressions and CTR compared with reference campaigns. This is a vendor case study, with its own context and metrics, and cannot be used as a direct cost comparison or as proof that compose is superior.

The two examples share one point: capabilities available in the market were integrated into a specific business process. The composition, however, took different forms, and the degree of internal control disclosed in each case is also different.

This distinction matters because the value of the architecture cannot be deduced from the list of products used alone.

How much does each choice cost?

It is common to compare a platform license with the token bill of an in-house implementation. Almost everything that makes the comparison defensible is missing.

For a TCO analysis, I would consider initial investment, licenses, variable consumption, integration, security, engineering, operations, evolution, support and exit cost over a defined horizon, such as 36 months. The cost of a shared team must be allocated consistently, avoiding charging entire expenses to a single application or ignoring them because they already exist in the budget.

The FinOps Foundation treats AI spend as a category that cuts across APIs, SaaS, cloud and owned infrastructure. Its unit economics guidance also separates resource-efficiency metrics (such as cost per token) from service- or business-related metrics (such as cost per resolved case).

For agents, I would add one requirement: measure the cost per task completed within the defined quality criteria. One workflow may use a cheap model and produce more rework; another may cost more in inference and resolve the request on the first attempt. In both cases, the bill alone tells only part of the story.

Since Itaú does not disclose IARA's internal TCO or the contractual prices of the models used, it would be inappropriate to attribute simulated build, buy or compose numbers to it. In the third part of this series, the economic comparison will be done through hypothetical scenarios, with explicit assumptions and sensitivity analysis.

The table describes possible tendencies, not a ranking of superiority. A purchased solution may offer better observability and isolation than a poorly implemented in-house one. A well-executed build may, in turn, reduce dependencies and speed up new applications. Compose only improves the situation when the organization can operate the interfaces between components.

How I would record an architecture decision

I would start with a well-defined business capability: users, expected volume, outcome, error tolerance, systems accessed, permitted authority and data constraints. Then I would separate mandatory requirements from comparative criteria. An alternative that does not meet an essential security or privacy obligation should not be approved merely because it has a lower price.

Next, I would compare the remaining options on the same set of tasks. The pilot should measure quality and cost per outcome, behavior under failure, recovery, integration limits and maintenance effort. The commercial analysis would include price, increases, minimum commitments, support terms and export conditions.

The final decision should be recorded as an ADR or architecture document: alternatives considered, evidence, assumptions, accepted risks, owners, estimated costs and a review date. A three-year horizon can guide the investment, but the architecture must be revisited when prices, models, products or business needs change.

This discipline also reduces a common problem: purchase decisions made from functional demos, without checking how the solution will be operated after launch.

A choice that must remain valid

The IARA case shows an organization combining internal components with external models in a context of significant scale and constraints. Its value for this discussion lies in the separation between access to AI capability and control over its use. The public materials support this observation; they do not allow inferring its real cost or reproducing the bank's internal architecture.

For other companies, the design may be different. A purchased product may serve an entire area. An in-house capability may make sense for a highly specialized process. A composed architecture may let the organization keep contracts and authority over actions while taking advantage of managed services.

I would treat the decision as a combination of business architecture, economic model and operational responsibility. Before fixing the vendor, it is worth deciding which capabilities must remain under the company's control, what it can operate with quality and which dependencies it is willing to take on.
