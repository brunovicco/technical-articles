A 36-month simulation comparing build, buy and compose, and what changes when we measure completed tasks, not just model calls.

In Part 1 of this series, I discussed which capabilities an organization should keep under its control. In Part 2, I examined what is worth asking when contracting an agent platform and why comparing feature checklists is usually not enough. The financial discussion comes now, after those choices: it only makes sense to compare alternatives when we are clear about the work each one has to perform.

A company can contract a ready-made solution and pay per conversation, build its agent on a managed runtime, or host models and internal components. The three alternatives can deliver the same apparent result in a demo. In the budget, however, each one shifts costs to different places: licensing, inference, integration, engineering, support, idle capacity or human work. The initial savings can disappear when volume grows, and a solution with the lowest cost per call can produce the highest cost per resolved case.

To explore this difference, I built a reproducible 36-month simulation, with explicit assumptions, three demand scenarios and a break-even point between alternatives. The numbers are entirely hypothetical. They are not a quote from AWS, Microsoft, Salesforce, OpenAI or any company mentioned. The interest lies in the method: which assumptions must be verified before using TCO as a justification for architecture.

A public case shows the scale, but not the bill

The case of IARA, from Itaú Unibanco, serves as a reference for the enterprise dimension of the decision. In the study published by OpenAI on October 16, 2026, IARA appears as an in-house governance and orchestration platform, making different models available to teams with internal controls. The study describes a legal solution that operates on more than 90 million documents and performs more than 2 million tasks per month, with efficiency gains reported between 20% and 80% in steps of the area's processes.

These numbers help explain why routing, observability and cost per task become architecture questions. But the publication does not reveal IARA's TCO, the inference contracts, the investment in staff or the unit cost of those tasks. Nor can the efficiency gains per step be automatically converted into financial savings for the bank. I will use the case only to contextualize scale, the simulation that follows represents a fictional organization and does not try to estimate Itaú's spending.

This care also matters when we analyze vendor studies. In GenAds, Mercado Libre combined AWS models and services with development carried out with Mutt Data. The public case reports operational and commercial results, but does not give the total cost of the solution or a financial comparison of discarded alternatives. Both are good examples of real applications; neither, on its own, allows reconstructing a complete investment decision.

Platforms sell different units

The AI bill is made up of meters that are not directly interchangeable. OpenAI publishes prices per million input, output and cached input tokens, with distinct processing modalities. In Amazon Bedrock AgentCore, features such as runtime, gateway and memory use their own meters (for example, compute per vCPU-hour and memory per GB-hour, gateway invocations and memory operations).

Google Cloud also presents billing for agent compute and memory, with additional components for gateway and memory. In Salesforce Agentforce, the company can contract units through action credits, per conversation or under other commercial modalities. Microsoft Foundry distinguishes configured agents, with costs tied to models and tools, from hosted agents, which include container compute.

The same request can produce one conversation, five actions, several model calls, index queries, runtime sessions and a human review. Comparing one vendor's price per conversation with another's price per million tokens amounts to comparing units before measuring the process that connects them.

The market changes quickly even in the granularity of these meters. On October 12, 2026, AWS announced a change in the billing of AgentCore's short-term memory, replacing the fixed price per event with ingestion, retrieval and storage, with minimum billable sizes per event. An estimate based on events without checking the current modality could be wrong before the negotiation even starts.

What goes into the TCO of a capability

To evaluate build, buy and compose, I would separate deployment costs, recurring fixed costs and variable costs. The breakdown helps avoid two common mistakes: forgetting expenses that appear after the pilot and counting the same team or contract twice.

Deployment includes discovery, solution design, integration with systems, hardening, testing, acceptance, data migration and initial training. A company can take advantage of services it already has, but that reuse must be recognized correctly: an existing integration is not free if it generates maintenance or new demand for another team.

Recurring fixed costs include minimum licenses, platform operations, SRE, security, support, vendor management, monitoring and base infrastructure. For in-house workloads, hardware or contracted capacity must also be considered even when usage fluctuates. In the financial model, the treatment of prepaid contracts must be consistent with the period analyzed.

Variable costs follow volume: inference, tools, searches, incremental storage, document processing, data transfer, memory, billable actions and additional capacity. Part of this variation is not linear; batches, concurrency peaks, caching, commercial tiers and quotas can change the curve.

Finally, there are costs related to the outcome: human review, response correction, task repetition, support intervention and incident remediation. It is important to decide which of them are already accounted for in the operational component to avoid double counting. In the simulation, I keep human review outside the base scenario and then add it as a sensitivity test.

A useful formula to organize the model is:

36-month TCO = deployment + Σ(monthly fixed cost + monthly variable cost + additional operating costs).

The comparison below is nominal, with no discounting to present value, taxes or exchange-rate variation. This makes it possible to see the architectural differences, but it does not replace the organization's final financial analysis. To approve an investment, Finance and Procurement would need to incorporate these conditions, minimum commitments, price increases and cost of capital.

A simulation with growth over three years

Consider a company that wants to automate the preparation and initial analysis of internal requests. Each task receives documents, queries systems, validates rules and produces a structured result, possibly with referral to human review. The economic unit is a request initiated, not a token or a tool call. The quality requirement and the set of tasks are identical for the three architectures in the first cut.

I define illustrative financial assumptions, the same for the whole exercise:

Hypothetical assumptionBuyComposeBuild
Initial investmentR$ 120 thousandR$ 300 thousandR$ 650 thousand
Monthly fixed costR$ 20 thousandR$ 50 thousandR$ 100 thousand
Variable cost per requestR$ 0.55R$ 0.27R$ 0.08

In the buy scenario, the company contracts a more complete solution and takes on less in-house implementation. In compose, it combines managed services with its own integration and control layers. In build, it takes on a larger share of development and operations, with a hypothetically lower variable cost. These values should not be read as typical market behavior; they were chosen to test the relationship between initial investment, fixed cost and scale.

Demand growth is gradual:

At the end of three years, the cumulative cost is R$ 4.14 million for buy, R$ 3.72 million for compose and R$ 4.73 million for build. The result comes from a simple sum: initial investment + 36 months of fixed cost + six million times the variable cost.

Compose has the lowest cumulative cost in this scenario. The interpretation needs to consider when that advantage appears: in the first year, buy costs R$ 690 thousand, compose R$ 1.062 million and build R$ 1.898 million. The option with the largest initial outlay requires more confidence in later demand growth. For companies still validating a product, that exposure can weigh as much as the total TCO.

Volume can invert the choice

A simple way to test the robustness of the conclusion is to keep the prices and multiply all the scenario's volumes by 0.5 or 2. In this exercise, fixed and initial costs stay the same and there is no capacity constraint, a hypothesis favorable to linear models, which must be replaced with real curves after the pilot.

Demand scenario over 36 monthsBuyComposeBuildLowest TCO
3 million requestsR$ 2.49 MR$ 2.91 MR$ 4.49 MBuy
6 million requestsR$ 4.14 MR$ 3.72 MR$ 4.73 MCompose
12 million requestsR$ 7.44 MR$ 5.34 MR$ 5.21 MBuild

The first break-even point between buy and compose occurs at approximately 4.5 million cumulative requests. Beyond that volume, compose's variable savings offset its larger investment and fixed cost. Between compose and build, the break-even point is approximately 11.32 million requests over the 36-month horizon, all other conditions held constant. These are economic crossings of simplified functions, not guarantees that the infrastructures will support any volume without new capacity steps.

The exercise illustrates why the monthly average can hide risks. The same total of requests distributed in peaks of a few days may require reserves, queues, rate limits, scaling and capacity review. A runtime billed by active resources may respond differently from a GPU provisioned 24 hours a day or a conversation contract. Planning must incorporate concurrency, execution time and the profile of the peaks, not just the total number of calls.

The economic unit changes when the task must be correct

TCO per request initiated is useful for forecasting. To measure delivery efficiency, I would also track the cost per task correctly completed, with acceptance criteria set before execution. The FinOps Foundation treats unit economics and the cost of reaching an expected outcome as relevant measures for AI, including the need to separate successful outcomes from inadequate responses.

The formula must state the denominator:

Cost per completed task = total cost attributed to the process ÷ number of tasks that met the completion criteria.

Assuming, only to compare the base-scenario numbers, that 92% of the six million requests meet the criteria without additional rework, we would have 5.52 million completed tasks. The technical cost per completion would be approximately R$ 0.75 for buy, R$ 0.67 for compose and R$ 0.86 for build. The 92% rate is another hypothesis, equal for all options, not a measurement of vendor quality.

The conclusion can change if quality is not equivalent. To make this risk visible, imagine that buy reaches 95% correct completion and compose only 85%, keeping their nominal costs. In this test, buy would cost approximately R$ 0.73 per completion and compose R$ 0.73, with a slight advantage for buy when we use unrounded values. I am not suggesting that these quality differences exist in practice; the simulation shows that infrastructure savings can be cancelled by inferior performance in the real process.

The treatment of failures must be clear. A request rejected by policy, an incorrect response, a timeout and a human fallback do not necessarily represent the same kind of failure. Some failures are correct security behavior; others require correction; others shift work to the user. To compare platforms, the definition of completion must distinguish these cases and remain constant across the alternatives.

Human review changes the economic result

In the six-million-request scenario, the TCO difference between buy and compose is R$ 420 thousand. At a hypothetical incremental cost of R$ 4 per human review, 105 thousand additional reviews in compose would be enough to eliminate that advantage, if everything else stayed the same. This corresponds to only 1.75 percentage points of the total volume.

This is not an argument against human review, especially in regulated processes. It may be mandatory or necessary to reduce risk. The point is to account for its frequency and the type of work performed. When a platform requires more corrections or shifts more exceptions to specialists, that effort must appear in the financial analysis and in the quality indicators.

How to connect bills to a business outcome

In agent systems, one execution_id can generate several model calls, several tool queries and a runtime session. The cloud bill normally knows the SKU, price, resource and account, but does not necessarily know which business request originated the expense.

I would build a minimal cost record assigning to each execution: consuming product or area, task type, workflow version, model used, number of calls, tokens per billing class, runtime resources, tools invoked, duration, outcome and human-review indicator. These data must be correlated by technical identifiers and protected so that observability does not turn into indiscriminate collection of sensitive content.

The FOCUS specification, maintained by the FinOps Foundation, helps normalize financial data across providers. The EffectiveCost field, for example, seeks to reflect the effective cost of usage after adjustments and allocation of commitments, avoiding confusing the outlay of an upfront purchase with its consumption over time. This does not by itself solve the link to business outcomes. The company still needs a ledger or attribution layer that joins billing records to application events.

This infrastructure makes it possible to answer questions that an isolated API bill does not: how much it costs to process a request per product, how many cases repeated a tool, which model generates more rework, what share storage and runtime have in the cost of a process, and what happened to the cost per task after a model change.

The cost of idle capacity and commitments

The comparison between managed API and in-house serving usually highlights the marginal price of inference. But reserved infrastructure requires different treatment. A GPU contracted by the hour has a cost even when it receives no requests; a serverless runtime may charge for active resources, but may also introduce startup latency or concurrency limits; a purchase with a commitment can reduce the unit price and increase the risk of unused capacity.

I would separate three indicators: capacity utilization, effective cost per useful hour and cost per completed task. Utilization can improve with batching and workload consolidation, but should not be maximized while ignoring latency, SLA or isolation between customers.

In addition, the billing model changes during the life of the product. In November 2026, public prices show several consumption and commitment modalities. I would not use today's table to project 36 months without anticipating contractual revisions and sensitivity scenarios. For a new and variable workload, it may be rational to pay more per use during the learning phase; a capacity commitment only starts to make sense when there is enough predictability to use what was contracted.

From TCO to realized economic value

TCO says how much the alternative costs. To decide whether it is worth funding, we still need to compare it with the process it intends to improve.

A team can demonstrate a reduction in document preparation time. This represents a measurable operational capability, but its financial translation depends on what happens to the freed time: was there an effective reduction in expenses, was a hire avoided, did service capacity grow without hiring more people, or did revenue tied to additional cases improve?

I would separate time saved, capacity released and financial benefit realized. The first can be measured on a sample of tasks. The second depends on utilization. The third requires a verifiable hypothesis of cash flow, avoided expense or incremental revenue, discounting effects of quality and rework.

For a complete business case, the return metric can be formulated as ROI = (realized incremental financial benefits − total attributable cost) ÷ total attributable cost. When using total cost, it is necessary to include expenses and benefits consistent with the same horizon, avoiding recording as savings both the freed time and the avoided expense derived from that time.

The FinOps Framework recommends relating AI investments to business value, with frequent review of forecasts and funding stages, from proofs of concept to scale. This brings Engineering, FinOps, Product, Procurement and Finance to the same decision: what is working, what needs to be renegotiated and what should stop receiving investment.

What I would take to investment approval

An economically defensible architecture proposal should present the problem and the unit of outcome; the expected volume per period; quality and review assumptions; the alternatives compared; initial, fixed and variable costs; impact on capacity and SLAs; commercial limits and commitments; the exit strategy; and which indicators will be reviewed after the pilot.

The most useful number would not be the point estimate for 36 months, but the identification of the conditions that make the decision change. In the simulation, compose has the lowest TCO at six million requests. Buy would be more suitable in the low-volume scenario, while build moves ahead with higher volume and amortized fixed cost. A relatively small difference in the need for human review can also cancel the expected advantage.

I would end the analysis by proposing a pilot with economic telemetry from the start. The organization should be able to reproduce the cost per execution, identify which steps consume resources, measure completion rate and compare alternatives on the same tasks. Without this work, the business case remains based on list prices and hypotheses that no one has verified.
