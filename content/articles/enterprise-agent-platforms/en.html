A feature catalog does not reveal how much control the company keeps, how it recovers an interrupted execution, or how much it costs to complete a task. A proposed evaluation using current offerings and the public GenAds case from Mercado Libre.

In the first part of this series, I used Itaú's IARA to discuss what makes sense to build, buy or combine in an enterprise AI capability. The bank's public experience pointed to its own orchestration and governance layer that allows it to consume different models. That is enough to discuss control and composition, but not to reconstruct its internal architecture or estimate costs.

Now the decision moves down a level. An organization has already decided it wants to contract part of its agent infrastructure. It receives vendor presentations and finds practically the same vocabulary in all of them: runtime, tools, models, identity, memory, observability and governance. The comparison looks settled until the questions that affect production appear.

Does the runtime control resumption after a failure, or does it just restart the session? Does identity belong to the agent, the user or the service hosting the execution? Does a financial action denied by policy stay blocked if the gateway becomes unavailable? Can traces, checkpoints and records of external effects be exported? Does a model change preserve the results accepted in testing? And, when the flow uses tools from several services, which unit appears on the bill?

I would not choose the platform by starting with a matrix of checkmarks. First, I would try to identify which responsibility will be transferred to the vendor, what will remain in the company's domain, and what evidence shows the split works.

A real case of composition, and its limits

Mercado Libre's GenAds helps separate product integration from infrastructure integration. According to the case study published by AWS, Mercado Libre worked with Mutt Data and used Stable Diffusion and Claude 3 Sonnet on Amazon Bedrock, plus Amazon S3, DynamoDB and an API to deliver advertising assets. The process includes generating backgrounds by category, selecting products and assembling the banners. AWS reports more than 90 thousand creatives produced, deployment in seven countries and positive differences in impressions and CTR compared with the study's benchmarks.

GenAds should not be presented as a study of adopting an autonomous agent platform. The case documents a generative AI application and the composition of services and models for a specific process. Its contribution to this discussion lies in the choice of capabilities: the models and infrastructure services are external; product integration, selection and the advertising experience answer to Mercado Libre's domain.

The study does not present an audited comparison among all available alternatives, nor contractual costs or TCO. The results are reported by the vendor. Using it as proof that Bedrock is the best platform for agents would overreach the evidence. Using it to illustrate how platform decisions appear inside a real architecture is defensible.

The platforms are not selling exactly the same thing

The market now offers alternatives between assembling execution yourself and contracting a nearly complete solution. In November 2026, the products most relevant to this analysis represent different propositions.

AWS: modular components in AgentCore

Amazon Bedrock AgentCore brings together runtime, tool gateway, identity, memory, observability and other capabilities. Its documentation also distinguishes the managed Harness from the Runtime used to host agent code, with support for different frameworks, models and protocols.

Modularity is attractive for teams that already operate workloads on AWS and want to take advantage of IAM, networking, monitoring and other existing services. There is no need to replace the whole application to contract a given part of the infrastructure. The trade-off lies in configuring the boundaries: memory, gateway, runtime, model, tools and policies can generate multiple operational decisions and billing items. An architecture with separate modules requires defining who is responsible for the continuity of execution and for the effective authorization of the tool.

Microsoft: declarative agents or hosted code in Foundry

Microsoft Foundry Agent Service documents at least two paths that are useful to compare: agents configured by instructions, models and tools (prompt agents) and agents hosted from your own code (hosted agents). The integrations include Microsoft Entra, shared tools, evaluation and observability.

This design can fit well in organizations whose identity, collaboration and integrations are concentrated in the Microsoft ecosystem. It is necessary to distinguish what the platform administers from what the team still maintains: a prompt agent does not require the same packaging work as a containerized agent, and the execution and cost mechanisms are not the same. The commercial documentation states that native prompt agents have no additional creation/execution charge, but models and tools are still billed; hosted agents add container compute.

Google Cloud: identity and runtime governance

The Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform combines low-code and code-based development, Agent Development Kit, Runtime, Registry, Identity and Gateway, plus evaluation and observability features. The documentation describes policies applied at the gateway and specific mechanisms for agent identity and delegated access on behalf of users.

For companies that require explicit separation between user identity, workload identity and delegated authority, these elements make the proposal interesting. That does not remove the need to test the real path to the tool: it is necessary to verify what happens when the agent acts as a service, when it acts on behalf of the user and when a call crosses another network or platform. Another point is to check regional availability and the maturity of each feature; being on a product page does not guarantee that every component is released in the contracted region.

Databricks: agents close to governed data

Agent Bricks is described by Databricks as a platform for custom agents developed in code. The solution brings together Agent Runtime, durable execution through AgentKit and DurableAgentServer, memory, sessions, MLflow Tracing, evaluation, Unity Catalog and Unity Gateway. The vendor also describes its use of different frameworks and models.

The fit can be stronger when much of the process depends on tables, documents, models and policies already maintained in the Databricks environment. The counterpart is that an architecture centered on the data platform can add dependencies for applications whose main logic sits in transactional channels, CRMs or external legacy systems. The pilot needs to measure that movement of data and tools. It is also worth checking whether the required functionality is generally available, in preview or in beta, since the documentation itself presents components of the development experience as beta.

Salesforce: agents close to the CRM process

Agentforce uses components and metadata from the Salesforce platform, including Flow, Apex, data and service channels. This can reduce effort when the business flow already happens in the CRM.

The main trade is accepting the product's extension model and commercial terms. A process with many actions outside Salesforce may require additional integrations, just as a very specific workflow may depend on limits set by the platform. The public table presents credit-based, per-conversation and per-user modalities; the commercial units cannot be treated as equivalent to each other or to the tokens charged by APIs.

Your own SDKs and frameworks remain candidates

An enterprise evaluation would not be complete without the alternative of keeping the runtime inside the application. LangGraph, OpenAI Agents SDK and other frameworks can run on infrastructure chosen by the company. The OpenAI Agents SDK, for example, documents sessions, handoffs, guardrails, tools and tracing, but this does not automatically replace enterprise services for deployment, identity, approval, scalability, security and support.

This option can offer greater integration freedom, but also more responsibility for execution, updates, failures and operations. A managed runtime can be integrated with your own code; the categories, therefore, have overlapping areas and do not represent a contest between equivalent products.

The comparison starts at the operational boundary

The first experiment I would run is to draw where a call starts and ends. The user makes a request; the agent decides to query a tool; the external service executes an operation; the result returns to the application; the workflow records what happened. Each step may have a different identity, persistence mechanism and retry condition.

If the platform manages the session and the application keeps another workflow state, it is necessary to define which record determines how to continue. If the call returns a timeout after having created a ticket, the runtime needs a reliable way to distinguish an unknown result from an action not executed. A checkpoint, on its own, does not prove that the side effect did not occur. The solution may require idempotency keys, a ledger or a confirmation query in the external system.

The same goes for authorization. A gateway with authentication is a useful component, but it does not automatically authorize the agent to use every tool it can discover. It would be necessary to demonstrate the complete path between identity, policy and enforcement, including user delegation scenarios and control unavailability.

On the observability side, I would ask whether the organization can extract relevant traces and metadata without improperly copying prompts, PII or sensitive results. In some scenarios, the vendor's retention policy and capture mechanism have important implications: the OpenAI Agents SDK documentation, for example, states that default tracing is not available to organizations using Zero Data Retention (ZDR) on their APIs.

The flow above can be tested before signing a multi-year contract. It requires a small, realistic process, with tools under control and failure scenarios injected deliberately.

A scorecard that starts with mandatory requirements

Instead of assigning scores to vendors based on marketing materials, I would split the evaluation into three parts. The first consists of conditions that must be met for the solution to stay in the process. The second measures behavior in a comparable pilot. The third covers economics and contract.

Mandatory requirements. The environment must meet the policies defined for data, processing, access, security, operations and audit. The company must confirm supported regions, the effective privacy and retention configuration, corporate identity, audit trails, environment segregation and contracting conditions. A feature that is announced but unavailable in the intended plan or region does not meet a mandatory requirement.

Technical evaluation. All alternatives must solve the same set of tasks. The platform and the adapters change, while the data, business rules and set of cases stay constant. A candidate may need more in-house implementation to reach the same result. That work also needs to be measured.

Commercial evaluation. It is necessary to compare capacity, licenses, consumption, contracts, support, limits, price increases and exit costs. The result will be incomplete if it considers only the price of inference.

A weighting matrix that I would use for a first cycle, subject to discussion with the business owners, would be:

Dimension after mandatory requirementsIllustrative weightEvidence I would ask for
Functional fit and quality20%Common dataset, acceptance criteria, completion and failure rates
Identity, authorization and traceability20%Privilege testing, policy decisions and exportable records
Continuity, recovery and operations20%Timeout after side effect, restart, retry, deduplication and incident procedure
Integration and implementation effort15%Integration code, setup time, contracts and dependencies
Operating economics15%Consumption and cost estimate per valid task, including tools
Portability and exit strategy10%Data export, workflow migration and proof of replacement

These weights are not a certified methodology, nor a market ranking. They serve to document what an organization considers important. A final score would only make sense after testing and due diligence. A vendor can reach the same score by different paths; in those cases, it would be necessary to explain the trade-off, not just publish the average.

The pilot needs cases that a demo usually does not show

An assistant that answers five questions well may not keep the same behavior when tools fail. I would assemble a test set with common requests, ambiguities, use of restricted data and operational errors, preserving the same distribution of cases for all candidates.

Some experiments would be mandatory:

  1. Improper access. The agent tries to query a resource outside the authorized scope. Record where the policy was evaluated, how the block occurred and whether another route bypasses the control.
  2. Timeout after the action. A tool executes the external effect, but the response never arrives. Verify whether the system queries the previous result or repeats the operation.
  3. Session resumption. The execution stops after a checkpoint and is resumed. Confirm continuity, necessary memory and the absence of duplicated work.
  4. Model change. Rerun the same set of cases after changing the model version. Measure quality, tool calls and regressions, without assuming that API compatibility implies behavioral equivalence.
  5. Control failure. Simulate unavailability of the policy or identity layer. Verify whether the application keeps an authorized contingency route or halts actions that cannot be verified.
  6. Export and exit. Retrieve configuration, data, evidence and the code needed to rebuild the capability in another environment, with documented effort.

This lab should deliver a record of each execution with stable identifiers, policy decision, authorized actions, result, estimated cost and human intervention when there is one. For systems with sensitive data, the evidence must be sufficient without indiscriminately reproducing personal content.

There is an important difference between a feature existing in the documentation and solving the problem at the required level. A platform may offer evaluation but not support the particular set of rules an institution needs to validate. It may offer memory, but not the desired retention model. It may export traces, but not include the evidence of a completed external transaction.

The negotiation must translate architecture into contract

Some questions that seem technical should go into the RFP and the negotiation: which feature is available in the contracted region? In which environments can data be persisted or transferred? Can the customer export logs, checkpoints and data in a usable format? How does the vendor communicate breaking changes and feature retirement? Which SLAs and limits apply to the runtime and dependent services? Which support responsibilities remain with the company and which stay with the provider?

I would also ask for a map of the workload's charges. AWS publishes specific lines for AgentCore features, including compute and gateway calls. Microsoft distinguishes inference, tools and compute for hosted agents. Google publishes costs for Agent Compute and other capabilities, and Salesforce uses credits, conversations and licenses depending on the modality.

Since these tables have different units, I would avoid turning nominal values into a price scoreboard. The unit of comparison would be a valid task completed under the same requirements, with estimates of fixed, variable and deployment costs. The 36-month TCO analysis is left for Part 3; here it is enough to know what to measure so that calculation is defensible.

A good portability test should also be part of the contract. Exporting chat history as JSON does not guarantee that the agent can be transferred without modifying the application. Workflow state, checkpoints, authorization, tool call format, memory schema and integration with external services may remain proprietary even when the endpoint uses an open protocol.

What I would take to an architecture committee

I would present a short comparison, accompanied by technical and commercial evidence. First, the definition of the capability and the set of mandatory requirements. Then, the pilot results with the same workload, including failure and authorization scenarios. Finally, the expected cost, the main dependency risks, the operational capacity needed and a tested exit strategy.

The decision may result in a single platform, a composed solution or keeping execution in-house. Selecting a vendor for one layer does not oblige the company to adopt it for all the others.

Mercado Libre shows that an AI application can combine a partner, models and cloud services around a concrete need. Current catalogs, in turn, show that the runtime and governance layer has also become contractable. Between these two facts lies an architecture choice that deserves to be made explicit: which responsibilities is the organization prepared to transfer, which will remain under its control, and how will it verify this during operation?

A platform that reduces initial effort can be a good decision. Another that expands execution freedom can be too. To know which one fits, I would trust less the number of features ticked in the commercial material and more the behavior observed under the conditions in which the application will actually have to operate.
