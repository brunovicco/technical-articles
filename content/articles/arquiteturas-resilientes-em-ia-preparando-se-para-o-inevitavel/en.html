Months of AI development, thousands invested—and at the most critical moment, your system freezes. This is not a hypothetical scenario. It happens. More often than you might think.

I see many posts and training courses focused on creating agents, building prompts, consuming APIs, and building interfaces, but I cannot recall seeing approaches to a fundamental issue: resilience. After all, what happens when the AI service you chose simply stops responding?

This is not a hypothetical question. On June 4, 2024, three AI giants—OpenAI's ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity—went down simultaneously. Companies with critical operations were left in the dark for hours, exposing the risk of relying on a single provider. On December 11, 2024, OpenAI experienced one of the largest outages ever recorded on its platform, revealing the technological fragility faced by global companies, especially in innovative areas such as artificial intelligence.

These incidents highlight the importance of resilient architectures in AI solutions. By deploying local models, diversifying providers, and using orchestration gateways, it is possible to mitigate the impact of failures and ensure service continuity.

Resilient Architecture: A Layered Approach

To avoid those moments of panic, a system that remains operational even in the face of critical failures can be your strongest guarantee of stability. This approach follows three main layers:

1. Local Foundation

Lighter language models (such as Llama 3 or Mistral 7B) running on-premise. These models:

  • Do not depend on external connections

  • Answer approximately 70% of the most common queries

  • Process sensitive information without sending it outside the corporate environment

  • Serve as a guaranteed fallback layer when everything else fails

These smaller models are not as powerful as cloud models, but they ensure your system never goes completely offline.

2. Multiple Providers in Dialogue

The second layer implements abstractions that standardize communication with different AI providers. The application does not know the specifics of the OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or Mistral APIs—it simply requests responses through a standardized interface.

This approach makes it possible to:

  • Switch providers without changing the core code

  • Route specific queries to the most suitable models

  • Implement real redundancy, with automatic fallbacks

  • Negotiate better prices by avoiding exclusive dependence on one vendor

3. The Conductor: Central Gateway

The heart of the system is a gateway that orchestrates all interactions. This component:

  • Runs health checks every 30 seconds on all endpoints

  • Implements circuit breakers that proactively isolate problematic components

  • Balances load across providers based on real-time availability and latency

  • Maintains detailed logs that become the main diagnostic tool

A Powerful Ally: Intelligent Caching with Embeddings

A caching system that goes beyond storing exact responses. This cache:

  • Creates vector embeddings for every incoming question

  • Stores responses associated with those embeddings

  • Identifies semantically similar questions, even when worded differently

  • Returns cached responses for similar queries, drastically reducing dependence on external APIs (except when mutable database data is involved, for example)

This system not only saves API costs but also acts as a resilience mechanism that keeps the system responding even during complete outages of external services.

Designing for Failure

The most important shift in this approach is philosophical: stop treating failures as exceptional events and start considering them a normal part of operations.

Some practices that transform the way we develop:

  • Clean abstractions save weekends: When we decouple our business logic from specific APIs, we gain the ability to "change a tire while the car is moving." If a service goes down, you can redirect 100% of traffic to another provider within minutes.

  • Scheduled chaos testing: Regularly simulate failures in system components to verify that fallback mechanisms work as expected. If you wait for a real failure to test your plan B, you will probably discover that it has problems too.

  • Active monitoring and early alerts: Configure systems that detect subtle performance degradation before it turns into complete failure, allowing time for preventive intervention.

The Cost of Resilience vs. the Cost of Failure

Implementing this resilient architecture requires more initial effort and results in a few additional bills at the end of the month. You will need to:

  • Maintain infrastructure for local models

  • Manage multiple accounts and API keys

  • Develop and maintain more complex orchestration systems

However, these costs are insignificant compared with the impact of a system that fails during demonstrations for important clients, marketing campaigns, or seasonal peak periods.

Be Prepared

Generative AI is redefining how companies interact with their customers, both internal and external. As more critical operations depend on these systems, resilience is becoming a requirement rather than a differentiator.

Fortunately, the AI ecosystem is maturing. With increasingly standardized APIs and models that can run locally, a few hours of preventive engineering today can avoid days of crisis management in the future.

How have you prepared your AI solutions to handle the inevitable? Does your architecture have plans B, C, and D, or is it still at the stage of "it is okay to fail; it is the AI provider's fault"?

In AI, failure is not a possibility—it is a certainty. And when (not if, when) it comes, there are only two options: you are ready, or you are out of the game.
