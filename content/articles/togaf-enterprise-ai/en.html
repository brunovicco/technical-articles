How to move from isolated initiatives to an enterprise AI architecture with clear responsibilities, priorities and evolution criteria, without turning TOGAF into a sequence of documents disconnected from delivery.

An organization starts its AI program with twelve initiatives spread across customer service, legal, operations and engineering. One assistant queries documents, another drafts customer replies, a pilot automates internal tasks, and a few teams are experimenting with coding agents. Several projects work, but each team has made its own decisions about models, tools, context storage, logs and evaluation mechanisms.

Now leadership wants to expand the program. It must decide which solutions stay isolated, which capabilities should be shared, and which investments will let new applications reach production without rebuilding the same infrastructure. There are legitimate demands for standardization, but also a known risk: building a central platform so heavy that every team ends up depending on it to move forward.

This is the situation where I would use enterprise architecture practices. Not to decide the agent framework in advance, but to connect what the company needs to be able to do with the capabilities, systems, data, responsibilities and investments that will make it possible.

In Part 1 of this series, I discussed what is worth building, buying or combining. In Part 2, I examined how to evaluate commercial platforms. In Part 3, I modeled the 36-month TCO and the cost per completed task. Now the unit of analysis changes: we are no longer choosing a single solution, but planning how a set of solutions can evolve without losing coherence.

The twelve-initiative example is hypothetical. The capabilities, maturity ratings, schedule and criteria in this article are working proposals, not data obtained from a real company.

The public case that helps frame the decision

Itaú offers a relevant example of an enterprise platform serving different experiences and needs. In July 2026, the bank announced ia.i, a conversational experience built into its app, initially available to about 300,000 customers. Extending it to the rest of the customer base by the end of 2026 was presented as a goal, not as a proven result.

In October, a case study published by OpenAI described IARA as a platform built by Itaú for governance and orchestration. According to the publication, it gives teams access to different models with observability, token cost control and responsible-AI policy enforcement. One of the applications cited, in the legal area, operates over a repository of more than 90 million documents and runs more than 2 million tasks per month. The publication reports efficiency gains of 20% to 80% in steps of the area's processes.

There are two useful lessons for this discussion. The first is that the customer experience can depend on platform capabilities that never appear in the interface. The second is that solutions with different characteristics, such as a conversational experience and high-volume legal processing, can benefit from shared services without necessarily sharing all of their business logic.

These data points, however, do not reveal Itaú's complete internal architecture, capability catalog, rollout roadmap or enterprise architecture process. Nor do they show that the bank used TOGAF in this project. I will use IARA only as a public reference for a platform approach; the design that follows is an original application of TOGAF concepts to a fictional organization. The numbers are results reported by the vendor's publication, not an independent audit.

What TOGAF adds to the discussion

The 10th edition of TOGAF separates its fundamental content, including the Architecture Development Method (ADM), techniques, architecture content and governance, from guides for specific applications such as Business Capabilities, Business Capability Planning and Digital Technology Adoption. The Open Group itself advises adapting the method to the organization's context.

This point matters. I see no advantage in running a full documentation cycle for every assistant or agent. I would use parts of the ADM to answer decisions that now cross several teams:

ArchiMate can complement this work as a modeling language. The Open Group distinguishes the roles: TOGAF guides the method; ArchiMate represents structures and relationships among goals, capabilities, applications, technology and change initiatives. I would start with business capabilities

A business capability describes something the organization must be able to do, regardless of which team, software or vendor implements it. Handling digital requests, producing legal analyses or assessing risk are business capabilities. A model gateway and a tracing service are technology mechanisms that can support them, but they do not replace them.

This distinction keeps the capability map from turning into a list of products. In the hypothetical scenario, the goals could be: increase digital resolution of requests, reduce rework on documents, preserve authorization controls and measure the effective cost of tasks.

From there, I would distinguish two views. The first identifies the affected business capabilities: contextual customer service, document analysis, assisted operations, software engineering and risk control. The second gathers the AI-enabling capabilities needed to sustain those journeys.

For these enabling capabilities, I would use a simple diagnostic scale: 0 = nonexistent; 1 = experimental or per team; 2 = operational with partial coverage; 3 = shared service, measurable and supported. The scale is my proposal for the example; it is not a maturity model prescribed by TOGAF.

A difference between current and target does not, by itself, determine the order of investment. A capability can have a large gap and be of low urgency. Another can be relatively mature and still carry the main risk of a critical process. I would add criticality, expected demand, dependencies and change effort before ordering the backlog.

The current architecture must be described without caricature

It is common to draw the current state as a set of disconnected boxes and the target as a perfect central platform. This tends to hide what already works and the real cost of change.

In the example, the teams may already have good corporate authentication services, delivery pipelines, a logging platform, reusable connectors and some experience with automated evaluation. The redundancy between projects may also be justified: a legal solution has retention and review rules that differ from those of a customer service experience.

I would document the current architecture through four questions:

  1. Which flows serve the business objectives? Map the journeys and the external effects that are allowed.
  2. Where does the data pass, and who can access it? Identify sources, classification, custody, retention and authorization boundaries.
  3. What is actually shared today? Check real reuse of services, including operations and support.
  4. Where does one team's failure affect the others? Identify dependencies, capacity limits and failure domains.

The result may show that not every existing tool needs to be replaced. An organization may have mature identity and observability outside the AI ecosystem. The most reasonable decision may be to integrate them into the new runtime rather than buy another solution to do the same job.

I would design a target architecture with explicit responsibilities

A shared architecture has to make clear what is mandatory and what stays flexible. My initial hypothesis would be to create a small set of cross-cutting capabilities while preserving each product's domain and evolution.

In the product teams would remain the user journey, the task definition, the business acceptance criteria, the specialized integrations and the decision about which effects the application may produce. This reduces the temptation to centralize everything that involves AI.

In the shared platform I would concentrate reusable standards and mechanisms: governed access to models, authentication and authorization at common boundaries, a tool catalog, telemetry emission, evaluation contracts, cost attribution and execution mechanisms that justify reuse. Some capabilities can be commercial products; others, adapters or services built in-house.

In the existing corporate layers would remain the identity, data, integration, security, network and operations systems. The AI platform should use them, respecting their responsibilities, rather than create a second parallel structure without justification.

There is an unavoidable trade-off. Concentrating controls can make auditing and operations easier, but it also creates critical dependencies and possible service bottlenecks for the squads. I would separate mandatory standards from mandatory single implementation. An authorization policy can be common without requiring every application to use exactly the same agentic framework. At the same time, essential controls should not be avoidable simply because a team chose another runtime.

From the gap to the build, buy or compose decision

The map and the target architecture do not yet authorize investment. The next step is to translate each gap into a decision with alternatives and consequences.

In the article's scenario, the tool identity and authorization capability would be a candidate for a composed solution: reuse corporate identity, use managed mechanisms where they fit, implement delegation contracts and keep the business policy under the company's control. Building an entire identity provider for agents would probably add responsibilities without proportional differentiation.

For model access, a shared gateway layer can make sense if it reduces duplicated integrations and lets the same model, observability and cost policy apply everywhere. Building one in-house, however, would only be justified after evaluating what managed services offer, the number of applications and the cost of operating the solution.

In evaluation, the benefit may lie less in choosing a single tool and more in establishing common contracts: a versioned dataset, acceptance criteria, regression, a record of the model used and promotion decisions. Each domain remains responsible for determining what a correct answer means.

The roadmap is a sequence of dependencies and investment decisions

After defining the target architecture, I would organize the transition into work packages. Each package would have an expected outcome, dependencies, an owner, acceptance evidence and a review condition. The periods below are illustrative and do not represent Itaú's planning.

Transition 1: diagnosis and foundations (months 0 to 3). Establish an inventory of the use cases, data contracts, identity and model consumption. Define minimum responsibilities and select two journeys for comparable evaluation. The criterion to advance would be the ability to reconstruct who requested a task, which model and tool were used, which policy was applied and what result was produced.

Transition 2: minimum shared services (months 4 to 6). Deploy a catalog for model and tool access, common instrumentation, evaluation mechanisms and integration with corporate services. The first priority would be to remove duplication that already causes measurable costs or risks, not to launch a giant platform before there is demand.

Transition 3: controlled adoption (months 7 to 9). Gradually migrate or integrate new products, keeping a comparable group of applications to measure effort, cost, quality and incidents. A golden path should reduce the number of repeated decisions the squads make, without blocking justified exceptions.

Transition 4: consolidation and review (months 10 to 12). Assess actual usage, operational capacity, vendor dependencies and cost per completed task. Retire redundant components only after demonstrating functional equivalence and a safe migration path. If the promised savings do not appear, revisit the strategy before expanding the investment.

The gates for each transition would not be only technical delivery milestones. I would measure real team adoption, time for a new application to reach production, the incidence of policy exceptions, quality, failure recovery rate and cost per business outcome.

The roadmap may change before month 12. A commercial offering may become a better fit, a model may change inference needs, or the organization may discover that a given capability is used by only one team. The target architecture should guide the evolution without turning old assumptions into permanent obligations.

Who governs the change and who pays for it

TOGAF treats governance and change management as part of architecture practice, not just a final review of documents. In the AI program, this governance has to reach product, platform, security, finance and operations decisions.

The exact organization depends on the operating model, the subject of Part 5. What matters now is to avoid treating the platform as the owner of every outcome just because it operates shared components.

There is also a financial consequence. The platform's fixed cost can be absorbed centrally at first, while demand is still small and needs an incentive to adopt. Later, the institution can adopt showback or chargeback mechanisms, as long as it can explain the allocation criterion and does not artificially penalize applications that share infrastructure.

The FinOps Foundation advises relating technology costs to unit and business-value metrics, distinguishing resource efficiency from the outcomes achieved. On an AI platform, this means that cost per million tokens and cost per correctly completed request answer different questions. To decide on scale, I would track both.

Trade-offs a capability map does not resolve on its own

Even with a detailed diagnosis, some choices depend on context.

Central platform versus squad autonomy: the more standardization, the easier it is to maintain certain policies and tools. On the other hand, a platform team without service capacity can become the new bottleneck. The way out is not to abandon standards, but to define stable interfaces, self-service and an exception process with identifiable accountability.

Portability versus vendor-specific features: avoiding any proprietary service can be expensive and delay delivery. Using a specialized service deeply can be rational when the gain outweighs the cost of replacing it. The decision should record which components must be reversible, within what timeframe and with what impact.

Uniform governance versus differences between processes: customer service, legal and operations may require different levels of review, retention and autonomy. A platform should offer common mechanisms and allow specific policies, without pretending that all journeys share the same risk profile.

Upfront investment versus future reuse: developing a shared capability creates cost before all the applications use it. The reuse hypothesis has to be measured: number of active consumers, observed reduction in effort per integration and actual operational savings. Counting announced projects as guaranteed demand distorts the business case.

Ideal architecture versus incremental change: it is tempting to chase the complete design and postpone any benefit until the platform is delivered. I would prefer transition architectures that can serve a real journey, with expansion and rollback mechanisms. This does not eliminate planning; it makes its hypotheses testable.

What I would consider a good enterprise architecture deliverable

At the end of this work, I would expect to be able to answer, with short and current artifacts:

  • Which business outcomes justify the proposed AI capabilities, and how will they be measured?
  • What already exists, where is the largest difference from the target, and which gaps are actually a priority?
  • Which mechanisms will be shared, which stay in the product and which can be contracted?
  • In what sequence must dependencies be resolved to reduce risk and bring value forward?
  • How much does each transition cost, who takes over operations and which indicators authorize continuing, revising or stopping?

The most important deliverable would not be an isolated diagram. It would be a coherent set of decisions: vision, capability map, baseline, target, gaps, work packages, acceptance criteria and a review record. The Open Group provides examples of 10th edition deliverables to support this organization, but does not require every company to adopt an identical documentation package.

The IARA case shows, through public evidence, that a governance and orchestration platform can support different applications at scale. TOGAF offers a way to structure the questions that come before and after choosing such a platform. The methodology does not, on its own, prove the value of the investment; that value appears when the hypotheses about capability, adoption, quality and economics are confronted with how the solutions actually behave.
